> **In 30 seconds**: Most outside investment voices are wrong, because **everyone lives inside their own "cave"** — you can't move into his cave, nor he into yours, so why fight over who's right? The market's experts are mostly **academics who rarely succeed at investing**; the media is noise; and even **ChatGPT's investment advice is an "academic scribe" fed textbooks**. Rather than spending energy proving others wrong, **"don't listen, don't look, it doesn't exist,"** and put the time into **falsifying your own system**, confirming your own understanding is true — **believing what you believe and verifying what you believe is simpler, more practical, and more effective than verifying others.**[^1]

## Everyone is in their own cave (Plato's cave)

James borrows Plato's allegory of the cave: most people spend their lives inside their own cave, never climbing out to see the world outside. Given that —[^1]

- **There's no need to argue with the outside over who's right.** "You can't move into his cave, and he can't move into yours, so why listen to what other caves are saying?"
- **Just stay comfortable in your own world**, don't mind outside voices — it's healthier for body and mind. Otherwise "someone says a few words and your mood is thrown off, and you want to fight them" — which is self-inflicted.
- This is the same posture as [[投資沒有標準答案|Investing has no standard answer]]'s "**don't challenge, don't argue, first say 'I could be wrong'**" — the difference is only that there it's humility toward *ideas*, and here it's decluttering *noise*.

## Why most outside voices can't be trusted

- **Systemic ignorance**: capitalist society "has never had proper financial education," precisely because it **doesn't want most people to understand** — that way everyone dutifully stays a laborer (see [[勞工還是資本家|Laborer or capitalist]]). So mainstream information is biased by design.[^1]
- **The experts are academics**: those who speak in the market are mostly academics, and "**experts rarely succeed at investing**"; James cites Buffett and Munger's "never study those (academic) people." Professors and financial media's "decadent noise" is even less worth hearing.[^1]
- **Even ChatGPT is an "academic scribe"**: "Even the investing advice you get from ChatGPT is an academic scribe — don't take it as truth." The logic is direct — **AI is fed textbooks**, and since academic investing itself can't succeed, whatever AI spits out from learning their playbook is equally useless to the ordinary investor.[^1]
  - **Exception use**: you can feed it CLEC's philosophy and restrict it to "explain using CLEC's investing philosophy" — then what you get is "our school's" answer; otherwise the generic ChatGPT out there "is just talking nonsense." 00575 a homemaker member uses it exactly this way to patch up what she can't follow (see [[過程才是老師]]).

> 🤖 **So is AI useless?** No — but use it differently: **don't ask it about what you believe** (it will agree with you); **ask it about what you don't believe** and make it show where you're wrong. In another chat room James went further — AI is a mirror that only reaches as high as your own understanding, and it tends to agree with you ("no loyal ministers, only a blind king"). See [[AI是一面鏡子]].

## The practice: don't listen, don't look, it doesn't exist — spend the energy "falsifying yourself"

James collapses information hygiene into two operable principles:[^1]

1. **Outward: don't listen, don't look, it doesn't exist — less information is better.** When someone sends him outside links, he "won't even look, won't even listen." Focusing on your own system already keeps you busy enough — "where's the time to mind what outsiders say?"
2. **Inward: constantly "falsify" your own understanding.** Spend the time checking "is there anything our system hasn't thought of or done well," and if so fine-tune and correct it, confirming your understanding is "true."
   - The key line: "**Believing what you believe and verifying what you believe is simpler, more practical, and more effective than verifying others.**" Trying to verify whether others' words are right is making trouble for yourself; spend the same energy calibrating your own system and it compounds.

> This is really the psychological cure for [[常見的投資錯誤|"trading in and out / always waiting a bit longer"]]: noise triggers action; cut the noise and only falsify your own system, and behavior naturally settles. Applied to the holding itself, James immediately returns to [[為什麼是納斯達克100|QQQ = owning 100 companies creating value every day]] — **just focus on those 100 companies continually creating value; the stock price and the market's voices have nothing to do with me**.[^1]

## Price is a voting machine: are you the noodle-shop owner, or the crowd shouting prices?

In 00576 a member asked a very concrete version: **the earnings were clearly good (cloud revenue up 37–82% year on year), so why did the price fall in the day or two after?** James's answer inverts what you should be looking at:[^2]

> **"Don't look at the price. The price is a voting machine — that isn't what matters. Look at what the company you invested in is doing."**

Then the noodle-shop metaphor:[^2]

- You run a noodle shop; you are the **shareholder**. What should occupy you is **cooking noodles for customers**, and what should worry you is **whether customers come.**
- **But outside, a few people keep shouting "your shop is worthless — it was worth 100 yesterday, today I'll only pay 50." Which one do you listen to?**
- The member answered it himself: "Look at the customers, because there are a lot of them." James: **"So many customers you can't cook fast enough, you can't take the money fast enough — why would you care about those people outside? They don't understand."**
- And flipped around: **"If it drops, isn't that a good time to buy?"** (See [[崩盤是朋友]].)

> 🔗 This is this page's "don't listen, don't look, it doesn't exist" applied to **earnings season**: **fundamentals (the customers) and price (the shouting) are two different things**, and you own only the first. In the same session James also demonstrated how to read fundamentals without reading price — he spent six minutes on three companies' cloud growth rates and forward P/Es, concluded "the market is healthy," and never once said whether to buy or sell.
