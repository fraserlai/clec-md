> **In 30 seconds**: A member shared that, after learning from CLEC, the deepest change wasn't a bigger portfolio — it was that **her relationship with her husband and her parents got better**. James turned that into an operating principle: **in social settings, the highest-level skill is not speaking**; at home, facing parents, a spouse, or children, **agreeing, accepting, and saying what the other person wants to hear is the safest response** — win the argument and lose the relationship, and your life goes dark. That's what he calls "**softness overcomes strength.**"[^1]

## A member's observation: better relationships, the biggest gift from investing

The member said James often reminds people to focus on three things — **relationships, health, and investing** — and that investing is, on this channel, already the "solved" part. What actually struck her most, and made her own life feel lighter, was that her relationships with her **husband and her parents** improved. She described this inside-out happiness as hard to explain to others, but very real.[^1]

## James's response: the highest social skill is silence

James agreed this was proof she'd already recognized what actually matters — no longer needing to prove herself right. He then offered a direct principle: **the highest level of social skill is not speaking.** He's noticed that in many social-media groups, there are always one or two people who feel compelled to argue and voice an opinion — "if they don't chime in, they genuinely can't stand it" — and **we shouldn't become that kind of person**. "Not responding is the best response"; in many settings, you have every right to stay quiet — no one requires you to weigh in.[^1]

## At home: agreement is the safest answer

James made this principle concrete for family settings — facing parents, children, a spouse, **you can't stay silent forever, but you can go along with what they want, agree, accept.** He put it bluntly: **"The only thing you need to say is yes, good, agreed, you're great — that's the safest answer"**; if you want to say more, say what the other person wants to hear.[^1]

He then spelled out the trade-off directly: **"do the right thing, say the kind thing"** — that's what produces a good relationship, and a good relationship is what produces a good mood, good health, and eventually wealth follows naturally. Conversely — **"you win the argument, you lose the relationship, and your life goes dark."** That is "**softness overcomes strength**": not arguing, not speaking, looks like weakness but is actually the strongest position; being able to agree with someone is a form of magnanimity and growth, not a low-level act of confrontation.[^1]

> 🔗 This extends the same worldview as [[孩子變大人父母變小孩|Kids Become Adults, Parents Become Kids]] — once the roles reverse and you start "managing" your aging parents' daily life, winning arguments tends to damage the relationship far more than simply not arguing at all.

## Reading the person who interrupts: let them talk, you don't need to win

James also offered a complementary communication skill: **being able to not speak is itself a capability.** He admitted he sometimes can't help himself either, but more importantly — **being able to let someone keep talking is a real skill**; if you interrupt them halfway through, you break their rhythm, and it becomes hard to tell whether it's you who can't communicate, or them who can't listen. When you run into someone who likes to interrupt, **let them talk and you get to stay quiet** — saying less is generally the better way to relate to people.[^1]

## What counts as "a big deal"? Cooking a meal or making coffee does

Extending this to everyday choices, James also redefined what counts as "important." It's not whether the government hands out cash or whether interest rates rise — issues that have nothing to do with him — **a wonderful lunch, a well-brewed cup of coffee, those are the big deals in his life**; if someone thinks spending time preparing a meal is a waste of time, he sees it as how he chooses to enjoy living. He also stressed — **this is what he considers important, not necessarily what a spouse or parent considers important** — whoever in the household wants to cook, or wants to eat out, that choice should be respected rather than forced into one "correct" answer.

## The highest good is like water: not contending is a strength, not weakness (00581)

In 00581 James put the whole of chapter 8 of Laozi's *Daodejing* on a slide: "The highest good is like water. Water benefits all things and does not contend; it dwells in places people disdain, and so is close to the Way… Because it does not contend, it incurs no blame." His reading:[^3]

- **The wise are soft — a trait of "confidence and strength."** Making your family feel respected doesn't make you weak; people respect those who can do it.
- **Cater to what they value**: if your spouse cares about cleanliness, care about it even more.
- **What's the use of fighting at home?** In the end it's a mess you have to clean up yourself — "it's not as if you can just leave once the fight is over."

### Ask before touching a family member's things

In the same session he explained "respect" with everyday examples: the half-finished drink your spouse left in the fridge, a pear someone else bought, the battered plastic bag the family told you not to use — **use it without asking, and the anger isn't about the drink but about "not being respected in this home"**; he even called that feeling "hatred."[^3]

- Ask before using anything that isn't yours; take a fruit out and ask, "Would you like a pear?"
- **What your spouse cares about, you should care about more** — regardless of whether you think it matters.
- **"Your spouse (anyone) is always right"**: to your spouse and family, always say "right," "OK," "yes"; outsiders you can walk away from, family you can't. Without respect and empathy, "you're not fit to be a spouse."
- Many people are deferential to outsiders and ignore their own family — **exactly backwards**.

## "The reason I have great troubles is that I have a self" (00577)

The 00577 deck quoted another line from Laozi: "**The reason I have great troubles is that I have a self; if I had no self, what troubles would I have?**" James reads "self" as **too strong an ego, too many fixed opinions**: insisting the market is surely at a top, insisting you're surely right, arguing with people every day — that's the great trouble.[^4]

- **"Changing yourself makes you a god; trying to change others makes you a lunatic."** Matters of understanding can only wait for the other person's own awakening — "a few days later they may find their understanding was wrong."
- **Strong inside, soft outside**: couples shouldn't debate; you won't win, and you can't teach them. **The one who doesn't argue is the strong one** — some feel "I didn't argue it out with him; am I a pushover?" No, you're the stronger one.
- Free of desire, one is unbending; softness overcomes hardness: "With no self, no one can hurt you."

## With aging parents: say "OK," don't try to persuade (00580)

In 00580 a member caring for their 80-year-old mother was repeatedly nagged about things already arranged, and even felt guilty. Speaking "as someone who's been through it," James said:[^5]

- **Getting angry at your mother isn't worth it — you'll often regret it.** Don't persuade or explain; if she wants you there on Sunday, say "OK, OK, OK"; your brother will show up anyway, and as long as things get done, that's fine.
- **"Silence is the best response; if you must respond, say 'OK.'"** Losing your temper hurts you and hurts her.
- He admits he doesn't always manage it himself — "over time you learn to let her words pass." (The practical side of caring for parents — hiring a caregiver, medication on schedule, protein — is on [[父母的老年財務|Parents' Late-Life Finances]].)

## No need to prove others wrong: lay out the data and let them decide (00580)

In the same session someone asked: when you push someone who insists on bonds or loves short-term trading and they blow up, doesn't that prove they're wrong? James: **we don't need to prove anyone wrong**, and don't needle them. At most, show them the chart of bonds falling 70–80% and staying down for ten years — **give them the information and let them judge**; if they still insist, accept what they believe. "**Wrestle with a pig, and onlookers can't tell which one is the pig.**"[^5]

## A Roman emperor's line: lie to the strong, tell the truth to the weak

Earlier in the same session, another member raised a quote for James to interpret — reportedly a Roman emperor's line, traced to a passage related to the *Odyssey*: **to offend a strong man, tell him a lie; to offend a weak man, tell him the truth.** The member connected this to James's teaching style — sometimes it sounds harsh (for instance, directly suggesting someone take an uncooperative relative to court), but on reflection, it's actually a very caring approach.[^2]

James admitted he hadn't studied that particular line, but responded with the story of **Confucius and Fan Chi**: Fan Chi once asked Confucius how to farm and grow vegetables, and Confucius said he was no match for an experienced farmer or gardener; but once Fan Chi left, Confucius remarked that this student was small-minded and didn't grasp the bigger principles. James's reading: **the same words call for a different response depending on the asker's mindset** — if someone is asking with a provocative attitude, you don't need to take it too seriously; teaching calls for **meeting the person where they are**, calibrating tone to the listener's mindset rather than applying one fixed standard answer.[^2]
