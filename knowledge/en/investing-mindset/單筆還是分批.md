> **In 30 seconds**: **Money you can invest goes in immediately at market price — and you never sell.** Don't ask whether to split it up, and certainly don't wait for a dip — "you're not a god! Waiting is the dumbest strategy!"[^1] Statistically a lump sum has a higher expected value than splitting; splitting only makes sense if you could **know in advance** that the market will keep falling for a year or more, and no one can.[^3][^4] But keep one distinction clear: **buying each month as your salary arrives (regular investing) already is "buy when you have money"**; taking money you already hold and keeping part of it on the sidelines to buy slowly is the market timing CLEC rejects.

## The rule: buy at market as soon as you have the money

The 00585 deck (also the fixed opening page every week) says it all:[^1]

> Money you can invest — buy it immediately at market price, and never sell! Buy and go to sleep. Which word don't you understand? Stop asking "is it OK to buy like this? Should I split it up?" And don't even talk about waiting for a drop to buy — you're not a god! **Waiting is the dumbest strategy!**

In 00585 he put it even more pointedly aloud: **"Choosing when to buy means you're a short-term trader"** — you only pick timing because you fear a short-term loss. The market was falling that week, and he asked back: "Isn't it falling now? If you're still asking me now, isn't that strange? **Afraid to buy when it falls, afraid to buy when it rises — then you'll never buy.**"[^8]

The same deck continues: **"According to statistics, a single lump-sum investment has a 70% higher win rate than market timing — so waiting is playing a game where the odds aren't on your side!"** And the cost of waiting is concrete — **you wait for a 10% drop, and the price you finally get is often higher than if you'd simply bought at market back then**.[^1] His real-world example: people waited in 2023, waited in 2024, and when the market finally fell in 2025 the price was still higher than in 2023; QQQ's 2022 peak was in the 300s, while now "however much it falls, it's still in the 600s."[^8] In 00580 he cited the same figure aloud: "If you buy at market as soon as you have money, your win rate is 70%; if you split it, hesitate, or wait for the market to fall, you're playing a game you can't win."[^2]

## The key distinction: "buy when you have money" isn't against regular investing

Many people conflate "lump sum vs. splitting" with "lump sum vs. regular monthly investing." CLEC's principle is **buy as soon as the money is in hand**, so:

| Situation | Is it market timing? | What CLEC does |
|---|---|---|
| Paid monthly, invest monthly | No — the money only arrives monthly | **That is "buy when you have money"**; do it |
| A retiree's unused monthly pension | No | Keep buying (see [[六七十歲才開始投資來得及嗎|Is it too late at 60 or 70?]]) |
| You already hold a lump sum (house sale, cancelled insurance, inheritance, savings) but feed it in over 12 months | **Yes** — deliberately keeping money out waiting for a better moment | Once the allocation is decided, **buy it all at market at once** |
| Waiting for a 10% drop, a pullback, or a premium to shrink | **Yes** | Don't wait (see [[大陸納指ETF溢價要不要等|the mainland-ETF premium question]]) |

The only reason to pause: **your allocation percentages aren't decided yet** — decide them first, then buy (see [[現金是空氣|Cash Is Air]]).

## The numbers: each has its own period and method — don't mix them

James has given different "win rates" at different times, and **they don't measure the same thing**:

| Source | Period | Figure | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Origins and Today*, chapter 44 (2023-06)[^4] | 2009–2023 | lump-sum success expectation **91%** | in 11 of 12 years, a lump sum at any time worked out |
| Post 0039 (2026-07)[^3] | 2009–2026 | about **97%** | only half a year of decline (H1 2022) in 17 years: 0.5 ÷ 17 ≈ 3% |
| Same | 2000–2026 (from the 2000 peak) | about **87%** | 3.5 down years in 27: 3.5 ÷ 27 ≈ 13% |
| 00585 deck, 00580 aloud (2026-08 / 11)[^1][^2] | not stated | lump sum "70% higher win rate" than timing | compares **lump sum vs. market timing**, not the lump sum's own win rate; no study cited |

The first three rows are James's own rough framework (declining time ÷ total time), **not a rigorous backtest**; the fourth is a different comparison. The single shared conclusion: **in an index that rises over the long run, the odds favor getting in earlier** (see [[統計概率隨機與意外|Statistics, Probability, Randomness]]).

## The only case where splitting makes sense — and why splitting is just as hard

- **Splitting only makes sense if you could know in advance that the market will keep falling for a year or more**; in an index that rises forever over the long run, such periods are rare and unforeseeable. Whether the market is topping, rising or moving sideways, a lump sum has the higher expected value.[^3][^4]
- **Splitting is just as hard as a lump sum**: when the market rises you want to wait for a pullback; when it falls you want to see if it falls further — and on average splitting does no better.[^3][^4]
- Counter-example: had you split purchases over twelve months in 2021, you'd have bought right at the peak all the way; splitting did no better than a lump sum then.[^3]

## How the stance evolved

It's the same road as [[從擇時操作到打死不賣|From Market Timing to Never Selling]]:

- **2018 (FB post) — still inside a timing framework**: "In a bull market, the earlier the money gets in the better; you shouldn't split." **Splitting was for a falling bear market**, when no one knows where the bottom is — "buying on dips." (The same post also recommended an actively managed Franklin technology fund to Taiwanese investors with little capital — an old approach he later abandoned completely.)[^5]
- **2022 (a bear-market year) — splitting allowed, waiting not**: "Buy when you have money; you can split it and buy slowly, buy more when it falls more, **but never think the market will keep falling and wait**." That year someone who'd sold a house asked how to invest the proceeds and he suggested splitting — not because splitting is better, but because all-in was too much for someone unprepared; splitting "only feels" less scary.[^6][^4]
- **2023 (*Origins and Today*, chapter 44) — lump sum wins statistically**: a 91% success expectation; "There's no perfect way to buy in the world… **how you buy isn't the decisive factor; holding still like a mountain is the point.** When you're most afraid, be brave!"[^4]
- **2026 (post 0039, 00580, 00585) — no more two answers**: the figures update to 97%/87%, and the weekly deck simply says "stop asking whether to split." In 00565 he explained why he's so absolute: in teaching, once you give two answers, everyone asks "which one should I use now?" — one begets two, two beget three, and it only gets more complicated.[^3][^7]

> 🔎 From "decide how to buy based on bull or bear" to "buy when you have money; how you buy doesn't matter; not selling is the mark of a master" (00455) — the same evolution as James's own path from timing to never selling.

## A "standard answer" isn't one everyone can swallow (00584 / 00585)

James also admits the answer has limits. In 00584: "Buy when you have money, sell the house, cancel the insurance, buy at market the moment you have the cash — **it looks like the standard answer, but can everyone actually swallow this medicine?**" Many people hear the show, sell their house, cancel insurance and go all in, then start doubting everything after three months of decline. "You have to weigh your own temperament… thinking you can doesn't mean you can, because you've never lived through the terror of a big crash."[^9] So he offers newcomers another tool, the "**three don't-knows**": buy at market, by dollar amount, and **don't know whether the market is up or down, don't know your purchase price, don't know whether your account is up or down** — "if you know none of it, you can last a long, long time."[^9]

00585 has a concrete case: a 24-year-old member planned to sell a Shenzhen apartment her parents had paid for, said plainly she **can't accept losing her parents' money** and won't use any leverage, and intended 30% in 513100 and 70% in fixed deposits. James first pointed out that "**fixed deposits are a guaranteed loss**," suggested moving the money to Hong Kong (where even USD deposits pay more than at home), then **moving into HK-listed Nasdaq-100 funds gradually — put in 300,000 first; if it falls, stop buying; once it's in profit, add more**.[^8] This doesn't overturn "buy all at once"; for someone who can't stomach a loss of principal, a staged entry steadies the mindset first — the same spirit as his 2022 approach for inexperienced elders (see [[六七十歲才開始投資來得及嗎|Is it too late at 60 or 70?]]).

## If you're worried a lump sum will lose money, the problem isn't how you buy

In 00571 a member called himself an investing novice who was very afraid of losses and asked: all in, split, or 433? James's answer: **"You should leave the market."** In 00580 likewise: if you worry about losses, don't invest — you'll end up selling at the bottom; "if you're afraid the plane will crash, don't fly."[^7][^2] That isn't a question of buying method but of [[為什麼是納斯達克100|who shouldn't invest]]; those who can bear it control volatility with [[現金是空氣|asset allocation]], not by splitting purchases.
