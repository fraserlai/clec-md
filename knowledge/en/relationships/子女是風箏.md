> **In 30 seconds**: CLEC often says — **investing can make you rich, but relationships decide whether you're happy.** James's central metaphor for raising children: **children are kites you don't tie down**; as long as the string of family love holds, a child naturally won't break away and knows the way home. And that string isn't tied by discipline — it's grown through **presence and example**.[^1]

## Education is "behavior education" — your example is the textbook

A child's earliest learning environment is the family, and **the parents' interactions shape the child most**. More crucially: **the child watches how you treat your own parents.** Whether you regularly visit, care for, take your parents to dinner, travel together — all of it quietly shapes your child's future relationship with you.[^1]

James's own practice: from the time his daughter was born, they returned to Taiwan every summer and winter to gather with family; he organized trips for the whole extended family of 15–16 (grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins). The result — **now his daughter proactively invites them to travel, and they eat, drink coffee, and chat together every week.** Quietly, that is teaching children how to spend [[快樂是人權|spiritual energy]].[^1]

### Before age six: security, hugs, and no fighting in front of the children

00578 pushes this example-setting back to the earliest years:[^8]

- **The years before six are critical**: a child's brain "learns its connections from the parents" — don't assume a baby of zero, one or two doesn't understand. Whether a child turns out happy, optimistic or insecure is largely set before six.
- **Hug them more**: the older generation said "let them cry, don't pick them up"; James thinks the opposite — "something must be wrong," and without a hug the child feels insecure. He held his daughter constantly and she grew up very secure — "I don't remember her ever crying," except ten minutes when she gave up her pacifier.
- **Don't fight in front of the children**: "Whether you end up divorcing doesn't matter; what matters is not arguing in front of the kids" — how can parents who fight constantly expect a psychologically healthy child?
- **Expecting filial children when you aren't filial to your own parents is climbing a tree to catch fish.** Dinner with your parents is for talking, "not for lecturing your parents the whole meal."

### No need for a school-district home: "children can't be taught into shape"

James keeps saying the family decides a child's future, not the school: "Stop shopping for countries' or schools' education systems… school districts, private schools, international schools, American schools — **none of that is what decides a child's success**"; the real key is **harmony and security** at home.[^8]

His reply in post 0043 is more concrete. A member raising a primary-school child asked whether to keep her home for its school district; James said the child doesn't need a school district — "**having parents around is already the best education**." His own parents never managed his studies — in primary school he bought a 500-problem *Applied Mathematics* book on his own, and he chose his university and major himself; his daughter in the US never went to cram school, he "never once saw her report card," and she handled her college applications, portfolio and recommendation letters herself. "**If parents don't over-compete, children won't over-compete.** Children are spoiled and mis-taught by their parents; they don't go bad on their own." And a very practical line: **how many families today can sit down to dinner together at one table? Doing that may matter more than any school district.**[^9] (Selling the home itself: see [[該不該賣掉自住房投入股市|Should I sell my home to invest?]].)

## Letting go of anxiety is the most precious gift

The title of one of James's posts is the conclusion: **letting go of your anxiety about your children is the most precious gift parents give them.** Schooling rarely nurtures a child to find themselves, discover an interest, and contribute to society. Rather than pass your anxiety to your child, give them freedom and example — the other side of the "kite" philosophy.[^2]

In 00577 he put it more bluntly: the lives of parents, spouses, friends and children are **their own lessons** — "you can't teach them." "Yes, he's your child — but he's also a passer-by." **If you can't treat your children as passers-by and insist on raising them as your property, it will end badly.**[^10]

## Steve Jobs, or a happy person?

A reader asked how to raise children. James turned it around: **do you want your child to have the chance to become a Steve Jobs, Bill Gates, or Elon — or to have a happy life?** He admits the American middle class is under great pressure; Taiwan's "small certain happiness," if you don't compare, can be quite joyful too. The point isn't which track you push them onto, but the **cohesion of the family**.[^1]

## Money buys the freedom to live and to be present

"As long as you have money, anywhere is a good place to live" — you can live anywhere, see anything. **Using money to buy happiness matters more than where you start out** (a home can change anytime; money is the freedom to live anywhere). A capitalist isn't bound by environment or others' values, and can be fully themselves anywhere. That ease lets you point your time and money at the people who truly matter.[^1]

**Emigrating: the whole family, or not at all.** In 00578 someone asked whether their child should attend an American school in Taiwan. James's framework:[^8]

- **First, have money (the freedom to live anywhere); second, keep the whole family together** — where you live follows from where the people are.
- If you really have NT$300–500 million and the child has a US passport, **start the child in an American kindergarten directly** rather than at an American school in Taiwan where parents compete over status — and retire and move the whole family.
- Without money, don't rush to emigrate: "I have no money, but I'll go to the US first and bring my family later" — **the family falls apart; why put yourself through that?** He estimates that for a Taiwanese investor-visa route (EB-5), NT$150 million isn't really enough and about NT$200 million is more workable; emigrating when the child is already 24 makes little sense.
- If the grown child wants to go abroad after university, "let them go" — and see if they have the grit; don't send them away young and get back a hand-out seeker (see the cautionary section below). Children need to grow up near their parents with **structure and rules** — "we're not redwoods in the wild that grow stronger on their own."


## After retirement, give your life to the people who matter

In retirement you **needn't keep up workplace relationships out of obligation** — give your limited life to those who truly matter: family, friends, yourself. This is the same as [[快樂是人權|"refusing mismatch"]]: you have the right to spend your time on the relationships that nourish you.[^3]

## The cautionary flip side: don't let money replace parenting

Opening 00574, James read a few letters asking for help and told the **inverse** of the kite philosophy — **replace presence with money, and you raise a devil**:[^4]

- Several families sent children abroad young as "little overseas students," and because they couldn't be present, **gave lots of money.** By their teens and twenties the kids became reckless spenders who treat their parents as strangers, even enemies. James's remedy is blunt: **an adult who won't respect themselves should leave and live independently** — "this is the parents' home, not yours."
- Some parents planned to "**just give each child $250k and be done with it.**" James's rebuke: **"Money is like a drug — give $250k to a child who can't control themselves and you're killing them."** A drug user only wants more; when the money runs out, they only demand more.
- His blood-and-tears example: his own **cousin**, sent abroad young with no supervision, fell into gangs, then drugs, returned to Taiwan, and finally took his own life. **"Don't fob a child off with money and think that settles your responsibility."**
- The bottom line: **"Better that money is merely enough — but a child's values must be right."** If the child is ruined, no amount of money you earn means anything.

> 🔗 This is the exact contrast to [[要不要讓小孩貸款上大學|"small annual gifts + teaching the child to invest"]]: giving a child money the same way, **a big lump sum to blow is a drug; small yearly amounts compounded in an index, with a child who already understands investing, is an asset.** The difference isn't the money — it's whether you taught the values first.
- **A college student racks up a six-figure credit-card debt buying designer bags — should you pay it off?** "Of course not — let him go bankrupt." (00582) But he draws a line: **a child with a congenital condition (ADHD, Down syndrome, etc.) is a different matter** — that child must be cared for well; it's a parent's mission; where special education is unfriendly, you might even scout out and move to another country (emigrating isn't easy and needs proof of means). With a healthy child, "you're already blessed — but of course don't spoil them."[^11]
- **A high-school granddaughter wants to drive a grandparent's Lexus?** In 00582 a retired US-based member wanted to lend their own car to their granddaughter and match the granddaughter's savings into QQQ; the member's daughter refused both. James sided with the daughter: a high-schooler in a Lexus doesn't fit, the owner is liable if there's a crash, and it's the mother's call; if your children don't trust your investing, "why bother scheming for them?" — **just get rich yourself and let them see it**.[^11]

## How much to give? "Enough to see it growing, not enough to coast"

A member asked the hardest question inside the kite philosophy: **if you invest for a child from age one and let them watch compounding, won't they conclude "mom's rich, I don't need to try"?** James's answer is an operational line:[^6]

- **The money should never be enough that they don't have to work.** What he gave his daughter was New Year money and birthday money — on her birthday, "let's go out to dinner, here's $500 to invest." That was it.
- **His daughter's actual numbers**: it compounded to **$60,000** by the time she started university (she said it wasn't even one year's tuition; he said never mind, dad will pay, keep holding it); **ten years later that account is in the $600,000s.** "For her, that money is **just enough to make investing feel good, but not enough to let her lie around doing nothing**" — and her own salary is high.
- **The counter-example**: "Not like the Taiwanese parents giving a child NT$2M a year, or $50,000 a year in the US — **you don't need to do that.**" (The same line as the "$250k = a drug" passage above.)
- **Two things must run together**: "one is **the education of life's values**, the other is **the education of wealth** — both at once." What he told his daughter: "What school you attend and what job you do don't matter; **you must work and support yourself** — go work at Starbucks, pouring someone a good cup of coffee is also serving people." Because **someone without spiritual energy can lose wealth even when they have it** (see [[快樂是人權|material vs. spiritual energy]]).

> The closing line: **"Don't let money poison your child."**[^6]

## When to start and how: let the account grow up with the child

The other half of that session is the mechanics — **timing matters more than the amount**:[^6]

- **You can start at four or five**: open a custodial account (Taiwan allows it too) and put **New Year and birthday money** into fractional shares. "At first they feel nothing, but then they notice **a few thousand suddenly became tens of thousands, then a hundred thousand**" — tell them "this can pay your tuition later" and they get it. By university it's NT$200k, by graduation over NT$1M — "**they realize: wow, how is that money growing so fast?**"
- **Why it only works young**: "They're a blank sheet — what you give them is all they have." Start at eighteen or nineteen and "**their head is no longer simple**" — they want a job and to earn money, and your NT$2,000 gaining under NT$100 a year gets "dad, what is this? Don't talk to me about toys."
- **Design the incentive as a share of their income** (he uses a manager's language): "Someone on a $100k salary — give them a $300 raise and they'll ask if you're joking. Someone earning $10k a month — give them $300 and they're delighted." So for **children already working**, the match must be meaningful: "if he earns NT$30k a month and you say invest NT$5,000 and I'll match NT$5,000, that motivates him; say you'll match NT$100 and he'll say **dad, you're joking, that doesn't even buy me a lunchbox**." — **different ages need different incentive schemes.**
- **Adult children get their own account**: once they earn a salary, "let them open their own account and invest themselves" — don't share one account.

> 🔗 This is the same system as [[要不要讓小孩貸款上大學|"small annual gifts + teaching them to invest"]]: **early, small, and visibly growing**; one big lump sum is a drug.

## Allowance: match the amount to the age, and match what they save

In 00578 a member's fourth-grader tricked his younger brother out of a NT$1,000 red envelope to buy spinning tops. The member usually saved the kids' red-envelope money into 00662 (the children tell classmates "I own 100 companies"), so she asked how much a child should be allowed to spend. James:[^8]

- **The amount must match the age — when it doesn't, money is a drug.** A primary-schooler who can decide alone to spend NT$1,000 is holding a drug; the day after the red envelope arrives, say "800 goes into savings, 200 is yours to spend however you like."
- **His daughter got US$5 a week in primary school**: she could spend it; whatever she didn't spend went into a piggy bank, and **he matched whatever she saved**. At New Year they'd open the piggy bank → bank savings (she saw a 0.35 interest rate — "why so low?") → an investment account.
- By college the account held roughly **US$60–70k**; he paid tuition separately and the account kept investing — **it's now around US$700–800k** ("I haven't even looked" — his spoken estimate, at a different point in time from the "six hundred-odd thousand" in chat room 0001).
- A NT$200 weekly allowance can work the same way: **let the child decide whether to spend or save, and the parents match what's saved**.

## An adult child who can't manage money (e.g. losing on day trading): be the backstop, don't hand over a lump sum

In 00577 a US-based mother's adult son had lost heavily day trading and was so stressed he feared for his health. James's advice:[^10]

- **Be a backstop, but don't give a large lump sum** — a little each month is fine; without income there's still Medicaid for doctor visits.
- **Give him a small account to "watch"**: each of her two children gets US$20k, then another 20k — a US$40k account each in QQQ, untouchable, so they watch 40k become 400k and 4 million. The point is training temperament — "watching the market swing and feeling nothing."
- **Only hand over an inheritance once you're sure he can manage money**; if neither child can be trusted, set up a **revocable living trust**. Even Buffett waited until his children were around sixty to judge them mature enough — so there's time to keep teaching. **Let them watch you: retired, yet the more you spend, the more you have.**

> A small aside — kids glued to video games? James's prescription in 00577: **take them outdoors** — hiking, Alishan, Hehuanshan, kayaking, ideally somewhere without internet. "Gaming isn't bad, but doing nothing but gaming isn't good."[^10]

## When the child won't take over the business or learn to invest — let go

A member close to retirement, a small-business owner, asked a painful question: the company still earns several million NT a year, but **his son doesn't want to take it over**. He even offered "put in NT$10k a month, I'll add NT$20k, invest the NT$30k for 20–25 years," and his son replied "**dad, I can't even afford to get married and you're telling me to climb a mountain?**" — preferring to spend NT$700k on a used car. He called it "a father's heartbreak."[^7]

James's answer is **to let it go**:[^7]

- **"Enough is enough."** "I was an engineer and later got into investing; my daughter doesn't like investing, she likes painting — nothing to be done."
- **Whether to keep the company is about responsibility, not succession**: even a small business has social obligations — for the people who depend on it, the question is "when it winds down will they find work, or should they be learning to invest now?"
- The member's own answer, which James endorsed: "I only learned from your videos that what I did was right — **I can provide you a platform and social resources, but not money.**" (Echoing "don't let money replace parenting" above.)
- His own realization was **decluttering (斷捨離)**: "I used to just know the phrase; when these things arrive in front of you, you learn what it's good for. **Children have their own lives; as parents we can only wish them well.**"

> This is the harsh side of the kite: **the string is affection, not control.** You can put the best method in front of them; you can't fly it for them.

### "Push yourself instead of pushing the kid"? — a child with love is happy (00585)

In 00585 a mainland mother asked: in such a competitive environment she'd rather "push herself than push her child," yet fears getting too lax — how happy should a child be? James: **a child who has love is happy** — babies are born smiling; if you notice the child has stopped smiling, something's wrong; it's worldly values that pollute children.[^13]

- **Parents don't strive for their children**: "You live your own life; the child lives theirs." Provide a loving environment without material want — not luxury, just food, a bed, and the occasional outing.
- **No need for elite high schools or international schools**: in the same session's deck segment he did the math — an international school can cost about a million a semester; **invest that money instead and your child can retire at graduation**. "As a capitalist you can do whatever you want."[^13]
- **Being there is the point**: if you can't relax, how can your child? He played chess, Chinese checkers, gomoku and tic-tac-toe with his daughter and built sandcastles at the beach — **what she reminisces about now isn't studying at all, only the times they played**. "Life really doesn't come from striving; it comes from relaxing."[^13]

## When your child (or anyone) is struggling: be there and listen — don't hand out answers (00583)

In 00583 a member said that after two weeks of solo childcare they realized "I thought I was shaping my child; actually my child was shaping me." James followed with a recent reflection: when someone near you is struggling — exhausted by childcare, a child with a congenital condition, trauma — **besides being there and listening, feel their pain**, and tell them "I know it's hard, I know you feel helpless, I'll be by your side." **Don't say "you're overthinking it" or "it won't be like that," and don't rush to give answers** — "even with your own child, don't give answers; we're not gods," especially on matters of the spirit, which have no answers anyway. When your child is hurting and frustrated, just say: "I know — it's hard, isn't it? You're angry, aren't you?"[^12]

## Encourage kids to spend — you're teaching them to create life experience

Paradoxically, while teaching a child **not to blow big money**, you should also encourage them to **dare to spend.** One student (in) shared how he balances restraint and permission: when his child clutched some money and asked "Dad, can I buy this $8 thing?", he said **"Anything under $10, stop asking me — if you want it, go buy it. Buy it, don't like it, then go find one you do like, and just keep doing that."**[^5]

James endorses the direction: **spending is creating life experience** (echoing the [[消費才是經濟之母|"spending is a virtue"]] lesson that day). What you really want to pass on isn't a sense of scarcity — that's often a shackle the prior generation accumulated in lean years and hands down unconsciously; once your inner life turns abundant, you realize **that scarcity shouldn't be passed to the next generation.**[^5]
