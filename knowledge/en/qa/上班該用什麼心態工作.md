> **In 30 seconds**: **Don't grind, don't sell your life.** Real ability at work is **making a big contribution in very little time** — a manager can be "idle" most days but must solve emergencies on the spot and see the company's risks ahead of time; **help your colleagues get promoted and prosper**, and when everyone needs you to get things done, "if you're not the general manager, who is?"[^1] Side hustles and startups mostly aren't needed: **put the extra money into investing and you'll have money**; if you can retire, don't start a business, and if you start one, don't quit your job — **unless you're creating something that doesn't yet exist on Earth**, just buy that industry's leader.[^1][^2]

## Situational variants (how people actually ask it)

- "What attitude should I take at work? (1) Clock in, collect the salary, save until retirement; (2) grind hard and climb to the top; (3) hustle on the side after hours, or even start a business?"
- "Should a young person start a business? Is opening a café a good idea?"
- "I can already retire — should I start a business now?"
- "In the AI era, can I build an app as a side hustle?"

## Three work attitudes, and James's take

In 00577 a member from mainland China laid out three work attitudes and asked James to weigh in:[^1]

1. **Clock-in mindset: the salary arrives, save enough, retire** — "it's not that you can't." The company can cut you any time, so there's no need to sell your life to it; just don't coast so far that you get fired.
2. **Grind hard and climb** — "no need." Whether you get promoted "isn't about working hard — I didn't work hard and I still moved up." What matters is efficiency and sharpness: **work with your brain, not your body**.
3. **Side hustles and startups** — "no need, because if you want more money, invest it and you'll have money." Unless it's a world-changing venture like Elon Musk's; if the thing already exists in the world, "most won't succeed."

## "I never sold my life": how James worked at Acer

James answered from his own career (see [[緣起與今日|his background]]):[^1]

- **In the US, of an eight-hour day he really worked two**; back in Taiwan running a whole business group, no meetings after 4 p.m., **out the door at 6 sharp**, and at 11:30 he'd go swim and eat, back by 1:30.
- **What engineers couldn't fix in three weeks, he fixed in an hour**: the day before, he had Taiwan send the parts drawings and the databook; that afternoon he took the papers and sat under a tree, "focusing all the energy on that one problem," and found the answer in half an hour ("lift this pin and the problem's solved").
- **A manager's value**: don't compete with your team for work; **solve emergencies immediately when they come**, and **see the company's future risks earlier than staff do and prevent them**.
- **Help others succeed instead of grabbing credit**: "When the whole company needs you to help them succeed — if you're not the general manager, who are you?"
- **Work for all of humanity**: he quotes "the purpose of life is to improve the life of all humankind" — making a product with no defects is working for every user; with that mindset your judgment stays straight and you don't get dragged into power struggles.

> In a word: **be a contributor — not someone who grinds, pulls 996, or works 12-hour days**; build real competence so you can solve a lot in little time and give good advice. That's ability.[^1]

## Starting a business: first ask, "Does the thing you'd make already exist?"

James has given the same answer for years:

- **2024 (00453)**: young people often ask whether to start a business; he asks back, "Does what you'd make already exist in the world?" If so, why start it? If you want your café to become a chain, "just buy Starbucks"; if you want to build phones, buy Apple stock. **A startup should create what doesn't exist**; following others isn't needed. He likens buying 00662/QQQ to "starting your own company — the moment you buy in, you have 100 excellent companies, an instant startup success."[^3]
- **2026-07 (post 0037)**: **if you can retire, he doesn't recommend starting a business; if you do start one, don't quit your job.** If your café's goal is to become Starbucks, put the money straight into Starbucks — "go inspect your company at Starbucks every day and drink your coffee"; if you want to be the next Microsoft or Apple, just buy QQQ (Taiwan's 00662). Only a venture that has never existed on Earth, for humanity's survival and exploration, is worth starting.[^2]
- **2023 (00441)**: AI lets anyone build an app, but "whether your thing is unique and whether you're an expert" matters more; uploading data without your own expertise isn't competitive.[^4]

## Once you have a cushion: work is service, not slacking (00583 / 00584 / 00585)

After learning CLEC and knowing they'll be rich, some people lose their way. Three answers from autumn 2026 fill in "how to work once you have a cushion":

- **Work is service, not optional (00585)**: a medical worker who'd gone all in on the Nasdaq said that apart from time with their baby they'd lost interest in most of life. James corrected a common misunderstanding: "It's not telling everyone to stop working… slacking at home, coasting at the office because 'I'll be rich anyway' — **that completely misunderstands us**." You may not want promotion or raises, "but you can't not want to serve others"; think from the general manager's height about how to save everyone effort — **do three big things for the company a day and you can go home**. Life without giving isn't happy; **learning and output (writing articles, organizing notes, explaining things publicly) are also sources of happiness**.[^5]
- **You have the right to say no (00583)**: a US-based member said a colleague reminded them "we have the right to say no," so they learned to negotiate work beyond their role with the boss. James: he too only learned in the US that you can say no — and change your mind; if you don't claim your rights, others claim them for you — though he added that what matters most is whether the work interests you and makes you happy; he himself used to love "minding other people's business."[^6]
- **Don't fear change; do three or more different jobs in a lifetime (00584)**: asked "if you were in your twenties again, what would you do differently?", James said he has few regrets — Acer was big enough that he moved from R&D to a US lab posting, factory quality control, sales and finally head of a business group, all by his own request. His advice to the young: **be brave about change**; transfer internally if the company is big enough, otherwise change jobs about every five years; "you should do at least three different kinds of work in your life for it to feel complete"; **always stay ready** — difficulties are what you grow from.[^7]

## How this relates to the "retirement" question

This page is about **your attitude while still working**; if you've run the numbers and are deciding whether to stop, see [[該不該辭職或提早退休|Am I qualified to quit / retire early?]] and [[別為錢賣命|don't sell your life for money]]. Both rest on the same base: **work is an option, not a sentence**. Life isn't about working or starting businesses forever — post 0037 closes with: after retiring, spend more time with your children, visit your parents, be with your spouse, and build your own way of thinking through reflection; "spiritual permanence is the happiest thing in life."[^2]
