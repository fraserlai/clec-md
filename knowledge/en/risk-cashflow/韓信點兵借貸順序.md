> **In 30 seconds**: When you need cash but refuse to sell stock, borrow **in order of risk attribute, not interest rate**. The order is **① personal loan → ② mortgage → ③ stock pledge**. The personal loan is lowest-risk, so borrow it first; if you have a house you won't sell, borrow against it; a [[質押借款|stock pledge]] moves with the share price and is highest-risk, so **save it for last** — use it to repay the earlier loans, pay taxes, and live. James calls this "Han Xin marshaling the troops" (韓信點兵) — deploy the safe money first.[^1]

## Why rank by risk, not by interest rate

In money management, **risk control comes first** — and borrowing is no exception: **whichever money is lower-risk to borrow, borrow it first; the interest rate is not the main consideration**. This is the opposite of the common instinct ("borrow whatever's cheapest"), but the logic is: use up the credit that *won't* turn around and bite you when the market falls.[^1]

| Order | Instrument | Risk attribute | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| ① | **Personal loan** | Lowest | Fixed limit and monthly payment; not subject to a margin call when stocks fall. If you can borrow it, borrow it first. |
| ② | **Mortgage / home-equity loan** | Medium | If you have a house you won't sell, borrow against it. Still part of the early "Han Xin" stage. |
| ③ | **[[質押借款\|Stock pledge]]** | Highest | The credit line floats with the share price; a big drop can tighten it or trigger a call. **Use it last**, to repay the first two, pay taxes, and cover living costs. |

## The pledge is cash flow, not fuel for a bigger bet

James frames the stock pledge as a **cash-flow tool** — in retirement or a temporary cash need, it generates cash flow without selling stock (see [[十五年現金流|15 years of cash flow]]), rather than leverage for adding to positions. Precisely because it is the highest-risk and is tied to your core assets, it belongs last, with the amount kept in check (paired with the "pledge ≤ 20%" principle of the [[三層防線|three lines of defense]]).[^1]

## Borrow starting capital beats saving small change

"Han Xin marshaling the troops" isn't only about deploying money you already have — it's about **using borrowed starting capital to accelerate investing**. 00571's point: **early retirement doesn't come from pinching pennies, it comes from making your starting capital bigger.**[^2]

- Investing NT$/$3,000 vs. 2,000 more per month changes when you can retire by **less than a year**. Living cramped now for those few months isn't worth it — **your present life and family memories can't be bought back with any amount of future money** (see [[別為錢賣命|don't sell your life for money]]).
- By contrast, **borrowing a personal loan (200k/500k/1M) as starting capital and investing it at once** affects future assets far more than saving an extra $500 a month. That's "Han Xin" applied at the *starting line*: borrow the safe money (personal loan) first to enlarge the principal.
- Post 0016's worked figures (starting from 200k already invested): investing **$3,000/mo** reaches 1M in about **7 years 3 months**, **$2,500** ~7y7m, **$2,000** ~8y3m — a few hundred a month is only a few months' difference; what really moves the needle is the starting lump.[^2]

> In one line: **borrow the starting capital big, rather than shave the living small.** But borrow it in the risk order above (personal loan before pledge), and within your means.

## First ask "can I repay it?": three repayment paths (00584)

Han Xin's ordered borrowing presumes you **can repay**. The 00584 deck turns "can repay" into a checkable condition:[^3]

> Only borrow money you can repay! **Borrowed money can't be repaid by selling stock**, so plan the repayment path before you borrow.

- **Three paths — meeting any one counts as "can repay"**: ① **amortizing** payments (paid monthly from salary or stable cash flow); ② **gradually accumulating cash so you have enough at maturity**; ③ **being confident you can refinance with new borrowing** (another bank will lend, or yours will renew).
- **Why not repay by selling stock**: "You say James says I'll make 15%, so I'll sell stock to repay — nobody guarantees that." You need 15+ years to be sure of no loss; if the market falls for 15 straight years after you borrow, how will you repay?
- **The interest-only trap**: common mainland personal loans are interest-first, principal-at-the-end (e.g. five years) — the interest is easy, but in five years you must produce the whole principal, and if you can't, you'll have to sell stock. So interest-only borrowers **need the discipline to accumulate cash gradually**, just holding it themselves; or be sure they can refinance then — "otherwise, if the bank suddenly pulls the line, you're in trouble." James's own preference: amortize if you can; it's simpler.
- **Priority: term > amount > rate**: "Borrow what you can borrow all at once and invest it! Don't borrow a cent you can't! … Given that you can repay, **the longer the term the better, then the bigger the amount the better; the interest rate isn't the point**." Circumstances differ by region — the final call is yours.

## Young people: start a year into your first job (00582)

The 00582 deck pitches "borrowing starting capital" straight at new graduates: **wealth doesn't come from salary but from assets working around the clock**; on day one of a job, what assets do you have? So **about a year into work you should take out a personal loan** — if your salary can repay it, you can borrow. "If you borrow 1 million earlier, you'll have 1 billion more than others."[^4] Why sooner is better, and why rates will only go up: see [[我適不適合借錢投資利率多高值得借|Am I suited to borrow to invest?]].
