> **In 30 seconds**: Growing up, our parents taught us what we could and couldn't do. As adults, we're the ones reminding them daily not to eat this, not to do that, to be careful crossing the road — **kids become adults, parents become kids.** James's point isn't a lament about which generation is more capable; it's that **how you now use a "raising a child" lens to look after your parents is the same lens your own children will eventually use on you** — an inevitable reversal every generation goes through, not a matter of who's right.[^1]

## One generation worse, or better, than the last?

James starts from his own observation: as children grow up, parents discover their kids may already know more, have seen more of the world than they have. There's less a parent can teach, and more a parent learns from their child. From the child's side, it looks similar: as kids, we thought our parents were always right; grown up, we find some of their decisions and views weren't quite correct. This pattern **keeps repeating** — the way we now see our own parents as "behind the times" is exactly how our own children will eventually see us.[^1]

James makes the process concrete: as kids, our parents taught us "how to use a phone, watch out for this, be careful of that, this door at the department store, don't get lost." As adults, the roles reverse — **we're the ones daily reminding our parents "don't eat that, don't do this, be careful crossing the road," and they don't listen any better than we did.** It's almost the same tone we now use with our own kids.[^1]

## Kids become adults, parents become kids

James sums it up in one line: **"Parents slowly grow into children; children slowly grow into adults"** — and we're caught right in the middle, **both adult and child at once**: acting as the adult teaching and caring for our children, while acting as the "child" managing and caring for our aging parents. This dual role is a stage every generation passes through.[^1]

He frames this as a **transition**, not a tragedy: "As it turns out, once the kids have grown up, we parents slowly become the ones being cared for." Parents once held our hands as we grew; one day, we'll watch, gratified, as our children walk a more brilliant path than ours — and maybe, one day, it's the child holding our hand as we walk.[^1]

James doesn't leave this as a pure family reflection — he ties it back to CLEC's usual order: **relationships, health, investing.** Whether you can calmly handle "managing your parents, managing your kids" is bound up with whether you have the financial freedom to be present, to cover long-term-care costs, and to make decisions — which is exactly why [[父母的老年財務|the financial authorizations before incapacity]] need to happen early, while your parents are still lucid and you still have the bandwidth, rather than waiting until the reversal has already happened and both sides are caught unprepared.
