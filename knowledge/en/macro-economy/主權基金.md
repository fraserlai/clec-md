> **In 30 seconds**: CLEC's view of national investing mirrors its view of personal investing — **surplus countries should set up a sovereign fund that invests in US indexes**, taking ownership of American companies, "beating them at their own game," with **the gains owned and shared by all citizens**.[^1][^4] An individual who saves 20% of salary into an index can retire, so "why can't a whole country retire after working 40 years?" — put 20% of tax revenue into a sovereign fund and everyone can retire in 40 years.[^3] James thinks the only country that truly got it right is **Norway**; the ideal isn't cash handouts but a **universal asset: everyone is born with a standard share of national assets whose yearly payout becomes income**.[^2]

## The original motive: a silent capitalist war (2005)

Post 0050 is an essay James wrote in 2005–2008 — the "original motive" behind his teaching. After retiring in 2004, he saw Asians working hard yet not as wealthy as Westerners, and concluded: **there's a silent capitalist war, and Asians and emerging nations have been enslaved without knowing it**.[^1]

- **Developed countries are the monarchs and nobles of global capitalism; developing countries are its commoners and laborers**: the former take most of the profit, the latter get wages and environmental damage.
- **Individuals face the same fate**: people who understand how capital works are like developed countries and reap the most; people who only earn wages from labor are like emerging nations, "forever just getting by" — **"This has nothing to do with where you live, and everything to do with your knowledge of and mindset about capital."**
- **The way out for a country**: invest FX reserves and tax revenue well — **a national sovereign fund invests in US indexes, gains ownership of US companies, and makes American companies and workers work for us**; citizens likewise put their earnings into US index funds long-term — the tenant farmer becomes the landlord.

> The essay reflects its 2005 context (through the subprime bubble), which James notes when reposting in 2026; the personal-level argument has since grown into [[勞工還是資本家|Laborer or Capitalist?]].[^1]

In 2022 he said the same thing: a sovereign fund is the state's business, and **emerging nations should become capitalists too** — "beat them at their own game, with the gains shared by all citizens"; individuals must learn to invest "so you don't become a laborer who gets exploited twice."[^5]

## Why can't a country retire?

James's favorite analogy treats a country like a person:[^3][^6]

- **An individual**: save 20% of salary into an index and you can retire after 18 years of effort (James's own experience).
- **A country**: "Everyone works 40 years and still can't retire… why can't a whole country retire after 40 years?" If 20% of tax revenue went into a sovereign fund, **all of Taiwan could retire in 40 years**.[^3]
- He said it even earlier, in 2021: surplus countries (with FX reserves and fiscal surpluses, like China and Taiwan) **should set up sovereign funds, and they should invest in QQQ**; "If I were mayor of Cupertino, in 10 or 20 years I'd have everyone in Cupertino retired — collect taxes, invest them, and eventually pay everyone out."[^6]

## The finance ministry runs it; FX reserves belong to everyone

In 2023 (00419) he cleared up two common misconceptions:[^3]

1. **A sovereign fund is set up by the finance ministry, not the central bank** — it uses the ministry's money, the taxpayers' money. "No central bank sets up a sovereign fund."
2. **FX reserves belong to all citizens — not the government, and certainly not the central bank** — but that doesn't mean handing them out. "If you hand it to everyone, the country goes bankrupt"; the reserves are for those who need them (e.g. companies buying overseas assets).

In 00579 a member made the same observation: when a government has so much money it **hands out cash to everyone, it shows it doesn't know what to do with the money** and lacks a capitalist's investing mindset; the member also described each person's own portfolio as "our personal sovereign fund."[^7]

## Why only Norway got it right

In 00577 a member shared Norway's example: its sovereign fund **holds about 1.5% of all listed companies worldwide**; with about 5.5 million people that's roughly US$400,000 per person — "for every 100 dollars the world's companies earn, 1.5 is earned for Norwegians." It began when oil was discovered last century and the government decided **not to spend the oil money but to put it in a fund that buys stocks worldwide**; oil runs out barrel by barrel, but global assets keep producing money; and **the government may spend at most 3% of the fund a year** (the member: "just like our 70/30"). The member's conclusion: people who say "come to the US and be our bag-holders" treat stocks as speculation; **buying the Nasdaq-100 isn't bag-holding — it's making Americans work for us**.[^2]

James's comments:[^2]

- "**Only Norway's sovereign fund operates normally and correctly**" — in his view Saudi Arabia's works for the royal family, and Singapore's Temasek "is probably working for pensions," not as common property of all citizens. (This is James's personal opinion.)
- "Every country in the world should be able to retire — why hasn't a country retired after a hundred years?"
- **Universal asset, not universal income**: everyone, whatever country they're born in, should own a standard share of national assets from birth; pay out each year, and the asset becomes cash flow, becomes income. If every country did this, all the better — everyone would be born with basic wealth, and "there'd be no K-shaped society."
- Why don't countries do it? "Politicians just aren't far-sighted enough."

## Another use: hot money can't inflate the market (2022)

In 00361 he described the defensive side: when foreign capital flows in to speculate on local assets, **a national sovereign fund immediately converts the money to US dollars and buys American assets**, so local assets don't get bid up and there's nothing to plunder when the foreign money leaves. "The problem is no emerging country does this"; the US doesn't need a sovereign fund — it prints money for investment banks to "conquer territory" (see [[美元霸權與石油美元|Dollar Hegemony and the Petrodollar]]).[^4]

## What it means for individuals

This page is about national policy, but James always brings it back to the individual: **if the country won't do it, do it yourself**. Put part of your income into US index funds for the long run ([[為什麼是納斯達克100|Why the Nasdaq-100]]) and you become your own household's sovereign fund — from laborer to capitalist ([[勞工還是資本家|Laborer or Capitalist?]]).[^1][^5]
