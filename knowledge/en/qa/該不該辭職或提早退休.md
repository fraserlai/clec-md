> **In 30 seconds**: Two steps. **First the math — "can I afford it"**: how many times your annual expenses you have, and whether cash flow holds ([[退休需要幾倍年開銷|the retirement multiple]], [[十五年現金流|15 years of cash flow]]). **Then the values — "is it worth it"**: once you hit 15–25×, the marginal point of working for money is small — stop trading your life for garbage ([[別為錢賣命|don't sell your life for money]]). Before switching to an easier job, run the numbers — often "keep working" and "retire" differ little; **full-time investing is fine but don't make it a new rat race**; if the workplace is toxic, leave.[^1]

## Situational variants (how people actually ask it)

- "Should I switch to a non-shift / less exhausting job, or just retire?"
- "Should I quit to invest full-time / live off investing?"
- "I'm already financially free — should I keep working?"
- "I've run the numbers and I seem to be past the threshold, but I just don't dare retire — what do I do?"
- "My company has a bad boss and toxic labor relations — should I leave?"
- "I have enough and planned to resign this month, but now the time has come I can't make myself do it — I'm afraid I'll regret it and can't go back. How do I get past this?"

## Two questions: first "can I afford it," then "is it worth it"

- **Can I afford it**: a pure financial question — see [[退休需要幾倍年開銷|the retirement multiple]] (15× is the threshold; the multiple scales with your high-dividend yield) and [[十五年現金流|15 years of cash flow]] (workers keep an unemployed-to-reemployed emergency fund; retirees need 15 years of cash flow).
- **Is it worth it**: a values question — see [[別為錢賣命|don't sell your life for money]]. James's stance is clear: past the threshold, dragging "an exhausted body" to work for money isn't worth it, because the surplus just becomes inheritance — and **living 10 more years beats working 10 more years**.[^2]

## Want an easier job? Run the difference first

A member in 00570 (42, a technician on traditional three-shift rotation) originally just wanted to switch to a non-shift job, ran a calculator, and found **the difference between working on and retiring outright was small**, so decided to just retire. That's the method in action: **rather than switch to another job that's still selling your life for money, do the math first — often you can already stop.**[^1]

## Should I invest full-time?

Yes, but see it clearly for what it is. A member in 00570 went from a 9-to-5 worker to a "free man," making investing their whole vocation, and finding it more interesting than the old job ("you can make money *and* make friends"). Another member could already retire but keeps working because it's interesting and helps people — just with less pressure and higher quality — **not for the money**.[^1]

The point isn't the title "living off investing," but this: investing itself **shouldn't become a new job to grind** (never sell, buy and sleep — it doesn't need you watching the screen daily). If full-time investing turns into another anxious rat race, it defeats the purpose.[^1]

## Toxic workplace? Leave

CLEC treats a toxic work environment as a relationship worth leaving. In 00570, a member looked back and saw how frightening their company's "traditional bad Taiwanese labor-relations culture, the boot-licking workplace culture" was. The principle matches [[別為錢賣命|don't sell your life for money]] and the "leave toxic relationships" thread — if the environment is toxic, get out fast. (This variant leans more toward the relationships side; see the toxic-relationships topic under Relationships & Family.)[^1]

## A common sticking point: you're already there, you just don't dare

For many people the barrier **isn't financial — it's psychological.** The whole 00546 session is titled "**You could have retired long ago; you just don't dare.**" James lowered the threshold from "50× annual expenses" to "**15× is enough to retire**" (10× in high-dividend paying ~12% for cash flow + 5× in QQQ / a 4-3-3 growth mix) and said flatly — **"If you want to retire, then retire; be a bit braver. You don't have to save up to 30× or 50×."** Once the numbers clear the threshold, all that's often left is the "I don't dare" hurdle — and that one is cleared by [[別為錢賣命|don't sell your life for money]] (ten more years alive beats ten more years working).[^3]

## Variant: you have enough but can't bring yourself to do it — then you're not ready (00583)

This was the theme of 00583. A member in the semiconductor industry wrote: they'd planned to resign this month, but now it's time they can't decide — nobody around them has done it; they worry they'll meddle too much with the kids and the household once retired; the current job isn't exhausting (they can still take the kids to and from school and spend two or three hours with them daily); the income is very good by Taiwan standards; and they fear that if they regret it they can't go back.[^5]

James's answer surprised many — **then don't retire yet**:[^5]

- **"If you're worried you'll regret retiring, you haven't met the conditions for retirement."** The right retirement mindset is like winning the lottery — when he retired early he "was so excited I wanted to grab a microphone and announce it to the world"; it's leaving the rat race with an "I'm free!" feeling — **confident, something you can say out loud** — not tiptoeing around, afraid of regret.
- **First be clear about why you want to retire**: if there's no particular reason beyond "I have enough money," that isn't a purpose — **"Having enough money isn't the goal, and even less a reason to retire."** Until you're clear, don't retire.
- All your worries are to be handled by learning and strengthening your spiritual energy; don't worry about "things two hours from now" or "things eight kilometers away."

> This is the third step on the same road as this page's "first can I afford it, then is it worth it": **affordable and worth it — and you also need to be clear what life you'll live once you're free**. How to start the days after retiring: see [[剛退休不知所措|Just retired and at a loss?]].

In the same session a newly retired member showed what "being clear" looks like: on the last day they **tap-danced out of the office** (colleagues said "such a happy creature isn't allowed in the office"); retirement isn't a race but a state — taking the kids to school can be unhurried, and there's finally time to read the health-check report properly. James's wrap-up was direct: retirement is a state, **reclaiming your own life** — life slows down, one thing a day. "**If you can retire, why wouldn't you want to?**" He likened it to the image from his childhood of an ox with an iron ring through its nose, led by a rope — "if you love being the ox, carry on."[^5]

### The opposite: able to retire but not doing it is "gambling with your life" (00582)

In 00582 a member shared that in 2023 they had only two or three million, borrowed to run 442 and later 433, reached **20×** this year and had just been retired ten days. James took the opportunity: **with Han Xin's ordered borrowing you can retire early — no need to wait for 33× or 50×; 17× and above is enough** — QQQI for living cash flow, the rest in 433 or 80/20, and in ten years it naturally becomes 33×. "**If you can retire and don't, you're really gambling with your life.**" As for "if everyone retires, who'll do the work?" — "What's that to me? The country doesn't look after us."[^6]

### Not there yet: you don't have to retire — work can get lighter too

- **00577**: a member at only about 10× worried about what happens to US accounts if they die, and asked when they could retire → James: "At ten times you still can't retire… talk about retiring once you're at 17× or more."[^7]
- **Post 0046**: a mainland student wrote that they used to fear losing their job and stayed meek in meetings; after following CLEC they stopped fretting, dared to voice different opinions in meetings — "the workload hasn't shrunk, but my mood has become incredibly light" — and even felt "**it's fine even if I never retire**."[^8] The confidence investing brings turns work back from a sentence into an option (see [[上班該用什麼心態工作|what attitude to bring to work]]).

## Variant: "a few more years of overtime and I can retire early" — is it worth it?

In 2024, 00493, a member asked a very concrete version: working a day job plus driving Uber on the side, "if I **stop pushing myself to work overtime** I can still get by, but **early retirement becomes impossible**" — hence the confusion. James didn't answer "yes" or "no"; he broke it into three things to compute:[^4]

- **First ask "how many more years until you want to retire?"** — without a target year there is no way to evaluate whether the overtime is worth it.
- **Convert the overtime into time bought**: "Another thousand a month might pull it in by under three years — **maybe from fifteen years to twelve**." Express overtime as **years shortened**, not a vague "retire sooner."
- **Then deduct your health**: "If it exhausts you completely … **sometimes your body matters more than anything**."
- **It isn't binary**: "Early retirement doesn't have to mean stopping — **cut back to 20 hours, 15 hours a week**, that's fine, and you can keep going to 65. That's good too — **just don't wear yourself out.**"
- **And he already has a base**: "The key thing is you now have **eight times annual expenses** — that's not bad, **at least you have a foundation.**"

> This shares the "can I afford it, then is it worth it" frame above, but adds the middle ground: **the answer needn't be "retire" or "don't retire" — it can be "cut your hours."** Compare [[別為錢賣命]] — the years bought with your health usually cost more than the years saved.

## Answers over time

- **2026-09-19 (00583)**: a member with enough money who couldn't bring themselves to quit → if you fear regret you're not ready — don't retire yet; retirement should feel like winning the lottery, something you can announce; first be clear why you want to retire — having enough isn't a reason. In the same session a newly retired member "tap-danced out of the office"; James: "retirement is a state — reclaiming your own life."[^5]
- **2026-09-12 (00582)**: a member just retired at 20× → 17× and up is enough; able to retire but not retiring is "gambling with your life."[^6]
- **2026-08-07 (post 0046)**: once investing stopped causing anxiety, work got lighter too — "it's fine even if I never retire."[^8]
- **2026-08-08 (00577)**: a member at about 10× → talk about retiring at 17× or more.[^7]
- **2026-06-20 (00570)**: a 42-year-old technician found switching jobs vs. retiring differed little → retired outright; another member went full-time-investing as a "free man"; another kept working after financial freedom, but for interest and helping others, not money. James echoed 00568/00569's "past the threshold, stop selling your life for money."[^1]
- **2025-12-27 (00546)**: the very theme is "you could have retired long ago; you just don't dare" — lowered the threshold to 15× annual expenses (10× high-dividend + 5× growth), "if you want to retire, then retire, be braver." Points out the barrier is often psychological, not financial.[^3]
- **2024-11-30 (00493)**: A member (day job + Uber overtime, already at 8× annual expenses) asked whether to keep pushing overtime for early retirement → set a target year, convert overtime into years shortened (15 → 12), then deduct health; or just cut hours to 15–20 a week and work to 65.[^4]

> 🔎 **The emphasis shifts by year**: 2024 (00493) is about **still accumulating — should you trade health for speed**; 2025–2026 (00546 / 00570) is about **already at the threshold — do you dare pull the trigger**. Both ends share one prerequisite — **run the numbers first** (see [[退休需要幾倍年開銷]]).

> ⚠️ "Can I quit" is a pure financial judgment that depends on your assets, cash flow, and local tax regime — this page is education only, not personalized career or retirement advice. Work out the numbers in [[退休需要幾倍年開銷|the retirement multiple]] and [[十五年現金流|15 years of cash flow]] first, then use [[別為錢賣命|don't sell your life for money]] to decide if it's worth it.
