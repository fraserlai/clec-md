> **In 30 seconds**: James's answer opens with one weighted sentence — **"Your mindset is extremely common!"** The sense of not deserving wealth isn't your nature; it's taught by **traditional Asian "pickle-jar" (醬缸) culture**: from childhood, parents drill in *"work hard at labor, don't get fanciful ideas — gold doesn't fall from the sky."* The view he wants to correct it with is — **"Being rich is a birthright! Happiness is a human right!" We were born deserving to be wealthy; that is the correct, normal view. A lifetime of labor is a mistaken belief and a shackle handed to us by society and family — it enslaved us.** Pursuing wealth, happiness, and well-being isn't asking too much; **it is everyone's basic human right**.[^1]

## Variants (how people actually ask)

- "I was born with a **laborer's fate** — surely I shouldn't cross into the 'capitalist' class this easily? It feels like silently owning a large share of society's wealth."
- "I'm afraid **heaven will disapprove** and send some disruption to break the plan."
- "I'm afraid that **whatever you gain you must lose** — that one small thing later (a policy change, a family emergency) will zero the whole plan."
- "Is this a **scarcity mindset** or **fatalism**? Is it common in investor psychology?"

## It's common — not a personal defect

The member laid out his plan clearly: dollar-cost average into the Nasdaq-100 long term, let compounding accumulate, expect a reasonable long-run return. His trouble wasn't the method — it was that **the more reason told him the road worked, the emptier he felt inside**. James's first line of reply is exactly **"Your mindset is extremely common!"** — removing the self-doubt ("am I unusually weak?") before answering anything else.[^1]

## The cause is "pickle-jar culture," not your fate

James locates the cause in culture, not in personal character: **traditional Asian pickle-jar culture is what produces the sense of not deserving.** Parents teach us from childhood — **"Work hard at labor, don't get fanciful ideas; gold doesn't fall from the sky!"**[^1]

This is the flip side of [[勞工還是資本家|"being rich is a birthright"]]: the education system's function is to **train competent laborers**, and **a capitalist doesn't need to be trained, only to be told**. So the sense of unworthiness is really **the fact that no one ever told you** — not that you fall short. On why the system leaves the road open but unadvertised, see [[資本家為何留翻身路會不會崩潰]].

## The answer: wealth is a birthright, happiness is a human right

James's own words:[^1]

> **"Being rich is a birthright! Happiness is a human right!"**
>
> **We were born deserving to be wealthy — that is the correct, normal view! A lifetime of labor is a mistaken belief and a shackle handed to us by society and family; it enslaved us!**

He then lifts the matter to the level of **rights** rather than of "deserving": "Teaching people to pursue wealth, happiness, and well-being is as it should be — every one of us is worthy of it! **This was always everyone's basic human right. You have always had the right to a wealthy, happy, fulfilled life.**"[^1]

In other words: **"do I deserve it" is a misplaced question.** In CLEC's vocabulary, wealth and happiness are [[快樂是人權|human rights]], not rewards — and a right doesn't require you to first prove you're worthy of it. For the other common form of self-enslavement (a laborer's body with a capitalist's head), see [[別為錢賣命]].

## Editor's note: the "fear of going to zero" half is a risk-control question, not a fate question

This post answers the **unworthiness** half. The other half of the question — "one small thing will zero the whole plan" — has its own prescriptions elsewhere in CLEC; this page just points the way (this section is editorial, not the post's wording):

- "Could a policy change or family emergency zero me out?" → a **cash-flow and allocation** question, not a luck question: [[十五年現金流|cash flow that survives the worst 15 years]].
- "Afraid a crash will break the plan" → [[崩盤是朋友]], [[投資的七個層級]].
- "Fatalism — feeling the outcome is decided by heaven" → [[統計概率隨機與意外|the long run is a falsifiable true proposition; only the short run is random]].
- **The way that doesn't run on willpower**: make it an identity question — [[身份認同與長期投資|"I am a long-term investor"]] — so never selling becomes natural instead of a daily tug-of-war with your own unease.
