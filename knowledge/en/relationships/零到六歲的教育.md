> **In 30 seconds**: A member just back from parental leave, with his wife home caring for their newborn, asked James whether it's already time to start giving the baby some educational stimulation. James's answer: **before age six, skip the skills classes — the top priority is security** — hold the baby, don't wait until they cry to pick them up. Next is **presence** and **being read to**. And stick to **paper books over iPads or phones**: before six, whatever the eyes and ears take in from the real world can't be replaced by any digital training — which is also why he thinks **we may be the last generation to have grown up with physical books.**[^1]

## The starting point: income anxiety during parental leave

A member shared that his wife took a full year of parental leave after giving birth. The government's roughly 80%-salary parental leave allowance was about to run out, and with no salary coming in, his wife was getting a bit anxious — could QQQI's dividends fill that income gap? James's answer was direct: **this isn't a question of "should I tap into investments," it's a question of "how do I pay a salary."** Just transfer money into her account as a "salary," so her account keeps receiving cash and she can spend it the way she did when she was working. Whichever account the money comes from — QQQI dividends or his own salary — doesn't matter; what matters is **keeping the rhythm of cash flowing in**, rather than letting the temporary lack of a paycheck become a source of anxiety.[^1]

> 🔗 This is another use case for the [[QQQI]] cash-flow tool — not just a retiree's withdrawal stream, but also a bridge for **a temporary household income gap**.

## Top priority before age six: security

The same member then asked: since investing is going to make the family increasingly wealthy, should the child start learning something early? James pulled the answer back to age-appropriate stages — **for a child under six, the first priority is security**:[^1]

- **A parent needs to be present, holding the child often** — don't wait until they cry to pick them up.
- He specifically noted: a mother caring for a newborn 24 hours a day is genuinely exhausting, and **the father needs to take over the moment he's home** to give her a break. Without someone to take over, caring for a baby alone is draining. He shared his own experience raising his daughter — once she fell asleep he got real relief, and he once drove her around the block just to get her to nod off so he could rest.
- This "breathing room" isn't just a matter of efficiency — **a caregiver's own sense of security directly shapes the security the child absorbs.**

## Second priority: presence and being read to

After security comes **presence** — playing with things the child can hold and move, and once they can't yet talk, playing soft music and reading storybooks aloud. James stressed: **don't assume the baby doesn't understand — when you read a story aloud, they take it in.** He raised his own daughter this way, reading her one storybook after another every day.[^1]

## Why paper books: irreplaceable stimulation for eyes and ears

James offered his reasoning for insisting on paper books, using an AI-training analogy: **today's AI needs huge volumes of photos, images, and video to learn to recognize something, but a human can identify a cat from just two or three photos** — even if only the cat's tail is visible in the frame, a person knows instantly it's a cat, something AI still struggles with. His conclusion: **the real sensory input a child's eyes and ears take in before age six matters enormously**, and no on-screen image or video can substitute for it.[^1]

Concrete recommendations:[^1]

- **Don't let a child under six watch iPads, phones, or TV** — it's not that the content on paper doesn't matter; "it doesn't matter, as long as it's on paper, anything works," the point is getting them to look and engage.
- **Take the child outdoors often** to encounter the real world, rather than handing them an iPad to keep them quiet.
- **Favor paper books, paper storybooks** — take them to the library to borrow what they want, or to a bookstore to pick out what they like.
- James even extends this principle **through high school** — stick with paper books as much as possible, because "this may be the last generation to still see physical books"; paper books will gradually disappear.

## Habits form young: a teenage granddaughter as a counter-example

Earlier in the same session, another longtime member (part of a circle of James's early students who still meet for coffee) shared a counter-example: his teenage granddaughter, whose library now lets her borrow far fewer books than she could as a child, has stopped reading paper books entirely — she only listens to audiobooks and reads AI-summarized content, reasoning that "everything's on the internet." By contrast, his grandson formed the habit young and always maxes out his library allowance. This confirms James's point: **the habit of reading on paper has to be built while a child is young** — trying to reverse it once they're a teenager is much harder. It's also why he recommends delaying exposure to digital devices (phones, tablets) — "getting to them later won't leave a child behind," while traditional philosophical thinking and the ability to ask questions are the more valuable skills going forward.[^2]
