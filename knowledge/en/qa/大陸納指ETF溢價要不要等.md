> **In 30 seconds**: **Ignore the premium; buy at market as soon as you have money.** The premium on mainland exchange-traded Nasdaq-100 ETFs comes from capital controls — "you'll never see the premium converge"; holding out for a discount means "you'll never get in this lifetime," and the gains you miss far exceed that 10% premium.[^1][^2] In practice, pick the channel by amount: **small monthly sums** can go into off-exchange funds (small premium but daily caps); **a large lump sum** goes into an exchange-traded ETF in one purchase.[^3] Buy **ETFs**, not LOFs; and if your money can legally move to Hong Kong, you can buy HK-listed Nasdaq-100 funds with no premium problem.[^4][^5]

## Situational variants (how people actually ask it)

- "513100 / 513300 are at a premium of over 10% — should I wait for it to come down before buying?"
- "The exchange-traded fund has a high premium and the off-exchange one has purchase limits — which do I buy?"
- "What's the difference between 161130, 160213 and 513100?"
- "Can I sell when the premium is high and buy back at a discount?"

## Why is there a premium, and why can't you wait it out?

James's explanation is simple: mainland QDII funds are constrained by FX quotas — **foreign exchange doesn't flow freely, so the industry can't get FX quickly enough to arbitrage the premium away** — which is why Chinese funds often carry big premiums, "and you'll never see the premium converge."[^2] Earlier he also said: "**It's at a premium because it's good** — it keeps rising, so people are willing to pay a bit more."[^6]

So his conclusion never changes:[^2][^7]

- "Friends in China, **stop asking me what to do about the premium**. The premium doesn't matter — buy at market immediately." Getting on board matters more.
- "You keep worrying about a 10% premium… **there may always be a 10% premium**" — if you never get on, you never make money.
- "**If you're waiting for it to turn into a discount before buying, you'll never get in this lifetime** — there's no discount for you."
- Trading on premiums and discounts: "that's all Wall Street jargon… anyone who tells you to trade on discounts is ignorant."[^6]

> It's the same logic as [[單筆還是分批|lump sum vs. splitting it up]]: waiting for the premium to fall is market timing.

## Exchange-traded or off-exchange? It depends on the amount (00580)

The 00580 deck lays out the two channels:[^3]

| | Off-exchange (subscription/redemption) | Exchange-traded |
|---|---|---|
| Premium | small | can be high |
| Limit | only tens to a few hundred RMB a day | buy in one go |
| Suits | monthly investing of 1,000–3,000 | a lump sum of tens or hundreds of thousands |

- **Small amounts**: buying a dozen or a hundred yuan a day off-exchange can absorb a 1,000–3,000 monthly budget; opening several brokerage accounts and buying daily may let you use up a month's quota.
- **Large amounts**: tens or hundreds of thousands through off-exchange "might take months or two years to get in — so buy the exchange-traded one, immediately." **It might rise 10% in a few months**, more efficient than feeding it in over three or four months, let alone two years. **"So don't worry about the premium."**
- Another consideration: concentrated accounts are easier to manage.

## Buy ETFs, not LOFs (00577)

In China, James mostly recommends **ETFs** rather than **LOFs**. For example, 161130 (E Fund Nasdaq-100) and 160213 (Guotai Nasdaq-100) are **LOFs** — tradable like stocks in a brokerage account, but different in nature.[^4] The deck lists exchange-traded Nasdaq-100 **ETFs** including 513100 (Guotai), 513300 (ChinaAMC), 513110 (Huatai-PineBridge), 159632 (Huaan) and 159501 (Harvest); to compare premiums and trading volume across funds, the deck recommends Jisilu, with Tiantian Fund for more complete data.[^4] In 2025 he also noted that China has twelve Nasdaq-100 funds: "this one has purchase limits, that one has a higher premium — no problem, just buy a different one."[^8]

## If you can move money to Hong Kong, the premium problem disappears

In post 0047 a mainland member in her twenties asked: the premium is about 12% — should she wait for it to fall? Her plan was 60% in 513300, 40% in a Hong Kong account buying 2× leveraged funds, and no cash (treating undrawn loan lines as her reserve). James replied:[^5]

- **"When buying Nasdaq-100 index funds in mainland China, ignore the premium."**
- **If you can move all your money to HSBC Hong Kong**, buy the HK-listed Nasdaq-100 index funds directly — "then there's no mainland-fund premium problem."
- On the allocation he suggested **30% in HK 2× leveraged funds and 10% cash** — "I don't approve this kind of allocation for everyone"; she could do it because she knows what she's doing.

> Taxes and estate tax on offshore accounts (CRS, US estate tax, UCITS) are a separate question — see [[全球納斯達克100指數基金對照|the global Nasdaq-100 fund guide]] and [[UCITS-ETF-非美國投資人|UCITS ETFs for non-US investors]].
