> **In 30 seconds**: A student assumed he needed **50×** annual spending (2% withdrawal) — i.e. NT$48M for NT$96k/month — to retire. James corrected him: **you don't.** You can consider retiring at **15×** annual spending; liquid stock beyond "15 years of annual spending" doesn't matter to your life — the excess is just inheritance. At NT$96k/month, **NT$14.4M (= 96k × 12 × 15) is enough.**[^1]

> 📌 This is an **important update** to the [[現金是空氣|four retirement withdrawal tiers]]: those "50× / 33×" tiers think in pure withdrawal-rate terms; this page (newer, 2026-06) uses **QQQI high-dividend cash flow** to slash the required retirement assets. Per "newer content wins," treat this page as the target number.

## Why not 50× / NT$48M

CLEC's two thresholds: **you can consider retiring at 15× annual spending; you'd only need 50× if you withdraw 2% without relying on dividend cash flow.** But once you bring in a high-dividend cash-flow instrument like [[QQQI]], you don't need to pile up to 50× — **liquid stock beyond 15 years of spending neither affects your life nor is necessary.**[^1]

## 2.5% is the dividing line: do you need high-dividend?

00542 gives a sharper test: **a 2.5% withdrawal rate (≈ 40× annual spending) is the dividing line for "do you need to add high-dividend."**[^3]

- **Withdrawal ≤ 2.5% (assets ≥ 40×)**: pure 4-3-3 / 70-30 / 80-20 withdrawal is enough — **no need to touch high-dividend.** "Within a 2.5% withdrawal rate, the asset multiple alone works."
- **Withdrawal > 2.5% (under 40×)**: a pure index allocation can't throw off enough cash flow, so you **partly substitute in [[QQQI]] high-dividend** to prop up the cash flow. That's high-dividend's real purpose — only for retirees with a higher withdrawal rate, not for the young (see [[年輕人適合買高股息嗎]]).

> 🧮 **The blend method (how to size the ratio)**: split assets into portfolios A and B and use their "returns" to hit your target withdrawal rate. A = pure index (QQQ / 4-3-3), yielding about **2.5%**; B = [[QQQI]], conservatively **11%**. To draw 4% or 5%, solve "A×2.5% + B×11% = target." E.g. to draw 2.5% you go 100% A (no B needed); the higher the withdrawal rate, the larger B's (high-dividend) share.[^3]

## The "3% rule" as a dynamic formula: why the money never runs out

The withdrawal rates above are "a percentage of a fixed principal"; 00335 goes further with the **3% rule as a dynamic formula** — **spending floats with your assets each month/year**, which is the real key to "never running out":[^4]

- **Monthly spending = last month's (or month-start) assets × 3% ÷ 12.** Good market, more assets → next month you can spend a bit more; bad market, shrunken assets → spending auto-shrinks. To avoid violent month-to-month swings in living costs, in practice you **reset once a year**: recompute this year's monthly allowance from the assets at the start of the year, and use it through year-end.
- **As long as you "never exceed 3%," it's very safe** — because you spend a *percentage of current assets*, not a fixed amount, so you automatically spend less when assets fall, and the principal is never drained. James: "Don't spend more than 3%; keep to 3% and it's actually very safe."
- **Borrowing beats selling**: to fund that same 3%, **pledge lending** ([[質押借款]]) is safer than selling stock — the principal is never touched and keeps compounding; you're just borrowing to spend (see [[欠錢不還與資產傳承]]). Over the long run, "borrow to spend" preserves assets better than "sell to spend."

> This is the same thing as the withdrawal-rate tiers above, told two ways: the tiers ask statically how many times your expenses you hold; the 3% rule dynamically lets spending track your assets. Both point to the same safety line — **don't let your withdrawal rate stay above 3% for long.**

## Margin-of-safety update: 15× → 17×, QQQI 10 years → 12 years (00575)

In 2026-07 James made a **margin-of-safety** adjustment to the "10 years QQQI + 5 years 70/30 = 15×" example above. The reason: a high-dividend instrument like [[QQQI]] pays a distribution that floats with the market — **if stocks fall 50%, QQQI's dividend shrinks by about 30%.** Pinning it to a bare 15× with 10 years of QQQI means that in a downturn, when the dividend shrinks, the cash flow falls short.[^6]

- **Use 17×, with 12 years in QQQI** (up from 15×, 10 years). The extra 2 years of QQQI *is* the margin of safety: **even if the dividend shrinks 30%, you still receive the dividend you originally needed.**
- **When the market doesn't fall**: the dividend exceeds what you need, so put the surplus dividend **into [[為什麼是納斯達克100|00662]]** (growth).
- **15× is still retirable** — just at 15×, bearing the risk of "dividend shrinks in a downturn, spend a bit less" (a NT$50k dividend might drop to NT$40k). "If you barely have 15×, then 15× it is, you just have to take on some risk."[^6]

- **00578 spelled out "15 or 17" once more**: 15× assumes a dividend yield of about 12% (actually about 14% at the time), which already carries a margin of more than 10% — **enough to withstand roughly a 30% market fall**; 17× **withstands a 50% fall**. If a 50% drop and shrinking dividends hit in your first year of retirement, a monthly 100,000 might become 80-odd thousand — **if you can live on 10–20% less, 15× is enough**; only if you "absolutely must have 100,000 a month" do you need 17×. "15 is fine too; 17 is just to answer a lot of people's worries."[^9]

> This is a **conservative-izing** of the same cash-flow logic: it doesn't change the "live on high-dividend monthly distributions" structure, only widens the buffer from 10 to 12 years of annual spending, to absorb the "downturn dividend shrink" risk that's specific to QQQI (mechanism in [[QQQI]]).

### Where "fall 50% → dividend shrinks 30%" actually comes from (00576)

In 00575 that figure was a spoken estimate; a week later in 00576 James laid out **the derivation**, which is what gives it a basis:[^8]

| Step | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|
| QQQI recent price range | US$41 – 57 | — |
| Price decline | (57 − 41) ÷ 57 | ≈ **28%** (call it 30%) |
| Monthly dividend range | US$0.531 – 0.657 | — |
| Yield at the market low | (0.531 × 12) ÷ 41 | ≈ 15.5% |
| Yield at the market high | (0.657 × 12) ÷ 57 | ≈ 13.8% |
| Dividend spread | (0.657 − 0.531) ÷ 0.657 | ≈ **19%** (call it 20%) |

**The observed relationship is "market falls 30%, dividend drops 20%" — roughly a ⅔ ratio.** Extrapolating to the stress case: at a **50%** market fall, 50% × ⅔ ≈ **33%**, so the dividend drops roughly **30–33%**.[^8]

So when projecting future dividends, **discount the peak dividend to 70%**: 0.657 × 70% ≈ **0.46**, giving a yield of (0.46 × 12) ÷ 57 ≈ **9.7%**. Conclusion: **estimating QQQI at a 10% yield is the conservative, safe basis** — and that is exactly where "10 years → 12 years of annual spending" comes from.[^8]

### Two concrete allocations: 15× and 17× (00576)

00576 also loosened 00575's "17×": **"So does that mean 15 years becomes 17? Not necessarily."** The binding constraint isn't the total multiple — it's that **the QQQI sleeve must be 12×**; the rest is spread according to your total:[^8]

| Total assets | QQQI | QQQ (00662) | Money market fund |
|---|---|---|---|
| **15× (workable)** | 12× | 2× | 1× |
| **17× (better)** | 12× | 3× | 2× |

- **12× QQQI is the floor in both** — the margin of safety belongs on the high-dividend sleeve, because that's the part that shrinks.
- **Extra assets go to growth and the cash cushion first**, not to more QQQI.
- "That's just how it is… if you can actually reach 17×, that's better."[^8]

> ⚠️ Note this differs from **tier four** in [[現金是空氣]] (100 × monthly spending in QQQI plus 70/30) by more than 10 → 12: **the remainder is no longer a 70/30 sleeve but a 2:1 QQQ-to-money-market split**. Per "newer content wins," use this table.

## Count your retirement assets at the **market high** (00576)

A common worry: **"I want to retire this year, but the market just fell 20% from its high and my assets went from NT$35M to NT$30M — can I still retire?"** James's answer is **yes** — because CLEC's retirement simulation **already assumes you retire at the worst possible moment**:[^8]

> The premise is "the month you retire happens to be the market top, followed by three straight years down, with a maximum drawdown of 80%." Since the stress test starts from the high, **your retirement assets should be counted at their market-high value** — otherwise you're charging yourself for the same risk twice.

**Method: restore only the equity sleeve to its high; leave cash untouched.** With NT$30M, a market 20% off its high, and a 70/30 allocation:

| Step | Formula | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| QQQ sleeve (current) | 30M × 70% | NT$21M |
| Money market fund | 30M × 30% | NT$9M |
| **QQQ restored to the high** | 21M ÷ 80% | **NT$26.25M** |
| **Converted retirement assets** | 26.25M + 9M | **NT$35.25M** |
| Annual spend (3% withdrawal) | 35.25M × 3% | **NT$1.0575M** |

- **Only the equity sleeve gets divided by (1 − drawdown)**; the money market fund didn't fall with the market and counts at face value.
- The rule works at any drawdown: "Don't say 'the market already fell 50% the year I retired, my NT$50M is only NT$25M, so I don't have enough' — no. You restore it to where the index was at its high."[^8]

## QQQI is a "transition product": 15 → 20 → 30×, then sell it off (a senior member)

In the same session a senior member added a useful framing: **QQQI / 3451 / GEPQ are essentially "transition products" — a 5–10-year bridge** — carrying you from "retirement funds just short" to "assets grown large enough not to need them," and eventually sold off.[^7]

- **Start (retire at 15×)**: 10× in QQQI for living, 5× in 4-3-3 (see [[聰明再平衡法|4-3-3]]).
- **After 5 years**: if that 5× of 4-3-3 doubles with luck → becomes 10×, total ~**20×**.
- **After another 5 years (year 10)**: that 10× of 4-3-3 doubles again, total ~**30×**.
- **At 30×**: you can **dispose of** the transition QQQI, switch back to 4-2-4 / 80-20 / 70-30, and live on [[質押借款|pledge]] withdrawals of 3–4% (see [[質押提領比例能不能隨資產提高]]), with stable and still-growing cash flow.
- **Corollary**: since products like QQQI get sold off in a decade or so, **estate tax isn't their main concern** — whether you buy QQQI or GEPQ, it's fine (inheritance mechanism in [[欠錢不還與資產傳承]]).[^7]

### How the exit actually works: sell, move the money to a home-country broker (00576)

The following week, 00576 returned to this framework and supplied the **exit action** and the tax dilemma it resolves:[^8]

- **The dilemma**: a non-US resident buying QQQI **through a US broker** receives the full dividend but carries **US estate-tax** exposure while holding; buying a local substitute instead runs into **withholding-tax** problems. Neither side is clean.
- **So treat it as time-limited exposure**: during the years when you have only 15–17x and genuinely need QQQI's cash flow, you accept the risk — **and you actively end it once assets grow.**
- **The exit point is around 33x**: the remaining multiples sit in the Nasdaq-100; if they roughly 4x over ten years that's about 20x, plus the 12x in QQQI = **30-33x**. At that point you **sell the QQQI in the US brokerage account and move the money to your home-country broker**; the US estate-tax exposure ends and the allocation reverts to the long-term [[質押借款|pledge]]-withdrawal structure.
- **The risk during those ten years is "nothing may happen to you"** - if you die while holding, the account is still inside the US tax net. James's mitigation is a **joint account**: either party can act, so if one dies the other can wire the money out. "It's just one more avenue" - a personal choice.[^8]

- **00580's summary of retirement standards**: 50× annual expenses → 80/20, withdraw 2% a year; 33× → 70/30, withdraw 3%; **minimum 17×** → 12× in QQQI for living cash flow and the rest in 80/20 as the growth engine; in about ten years it may reach 33×, at which point sell the QQQI and switch everything to 70/30 at 3%. Two regional notes: **US investors** owe capital-gains tax on selling QQQI — convert gradually using a married couple's 0% long-term capital-gains bracket; **mainland investors** lack a good domestic high-dividend product, so during the transition hold QQQI in an offshore account and, at 33×, move the money to HSBC Hong Kong for pledging (details: [[全球納斯達克100指數基金對照|the global Nasdaq-100 fund guide]]).[^11]

> This **supplements rather than overturns** "estate tax isn't the main concern" above: precisely because you intend to sell within a decade and move the money, estate tax isn't decisive when picking the instrument - **but only if you actually carry out that exit**, and prepare for accidents during the transition.

## Real resilience is "15 years of cash flow," not "15 years of cash"

With no work income in retirement, **compute from a market high** and ensure your allocation's cash flow lasts **at least 15 years**. Note: this isn't holding "15 years of cash" — it's having the **cash flow** to support 15 years.[^2]

- 80/20 @ 2%, or 70/30 @ 3%: the paper cash position may only cover ~**10 years** of spending.
- A **high-dividend (QQQI)** allocation: the 15-year cash flow comes from **ongoing dividends** — a completely different concept from "holding 15 years of cash."

James's extreme stress test — all at once: your house is destroyed by disaster so you must rent elsewhere, the mortgage still runs, the market crashes near zero, you lose your job, you fall seriously ill, and even your [[質押借款|pledge]] line gets cut — **do you still have enough cash flow to live?** That's what allocation is for.[^2]

## Regional variants (by high-dividend yield)

The required "multiple" depends on the high-dividend yield you can get. Example: China, ¥100k/year spending, 6% high dividend:[^1]

- High-dividend sleeve: 10 ÷ 6% ≈ **¥1.667M** (produces cash flow)
- Growth/cash sleeve: 10 × 5 = ¥500k (70/30 or 80/20)
- Required assets ≈ ¥2.167M ≈ **21.67×** annual spending

## How do you count your retirement assets? Taxable accounts count only "after-tax" (~70%)

An often-overlooked detail that changes your retirement number (00553): **when calculating "do I have enough to retire," assets in a taxable brokerage account count only at their after-tax value** — because to use them later, you'll owe capital-gains tax.[^5]

- **Rough rule: use 70%**: taxable-account assets count toward your retirement total at roughly **70%** (the real ratio depends on your cost basis and local tax rate).
- **Example**: a taxable retirement account of **NT$3M** counts as only **NT$2.1M** (3M × 70%); if you have another **NT$2M** elsewhere, your retirement total is **NT$4.1M**, and retirement spending should be **NT$4.1M × 2%** — not 2% of NT$5M. **Overstating your usable assets is the most common retirement-math error.**
- **The one exception: pledge, and never sell.** If you plan to live on [[質押借款|stock-pledge]] borrowing in retirement and never sell a single share of principal ([[欠錢不還與資產傳承|buy-borrow-die]]), then those assets **don't trigger capital-gains tax and count in full** — which is exactly why CLEC prefers "borrow to spend" over "sell to spend" (see the 3% rule: borrowing beats selling).[^5]

> 🔗 This complements [[美國退休帳戶只提撥Roth|account type]]: Roth withdrawals are tax-free and count in full; Pre-Tax / taxable accounts get discounted. What you can truly use in full and tax-free is Roth + the pledged-never-sold portion.

> ⚠️ Workers and retirees differ: a worker's cash position is an emergency fund (living costs from job loss until the next job); the 15-year cash-flow framework is for retirees.
